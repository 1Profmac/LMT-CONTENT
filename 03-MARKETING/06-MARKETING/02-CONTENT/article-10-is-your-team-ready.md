# Is Your Team Ready for AI Training? Here's How to Know

Before you spend money on AI training — before you book the room, send the calendar invite, or sign a contract — you need an honest answer to one question:

Is your team actually ready?

Not "are they willing to show up?" That's a different question. Ready means: do the conditions exist for this training to stick?

After deploying across 9 locations in central Texas — libraries, senior centers, and a veterans program — we've learned that readiness isn't about enthusiasm. It's about infrastructure. And it's knowable before you spend a dollar.

Here are 7 questions that will tell you exactly where your team stands.

---

## Question 1: Do your people have consistent device access?

AI training requires a device. Not just "someone has a phone somewhere" — consistent, reliable access to a smartphone, tablet, or computer that they actually use.

If a significant portion of your team or participants is sharing devices, using library computers only, or has unreliable internet at home, that's a logistics problem that needs solving before training begins.

**Ready:** Most participants have a personal device they use regularly.
**Not ready yet:** Device access is inconsistent or shared. (Solve this first — device lending programs, library partnerships, or hotspot access can bridge the gap.)

---

## Question 2: Have they used technology for any daily task in the last 6 months?

Email. A smartphone. Online banking. GPS navigation. Video calls with family.

This isn't a high bar. It's a baseline. Adults who use any technology regularly — even casually — have the foundation to build on. Adults who have fully disengaged from technology need a different starting point.

**Ready:** Most participants use at least one form of technology daily, even informally.
**Not ready yet:** Significant portion has avoided all technology. (Start with a digital literacy bridge program before AI training.)

---

## Question 3: Can you identify one person who will champion this internally?

Training programs without an internal champion drift. Attendance drops. Participants hit a question and have nobody to call. The program becomes optional by default.

You need one person — not a full-time coordinator, just one person — who owns this. Checks in. Troubleshoots. Reports results.

**Ready:** You already know who this person would be.
**Not ready yet:** Nobody has been identified or assigned. (This is a 10-minute conversation with your team lead — have it before you launch.)

---

## Question 4: Is there a real problem the training will solve?

Generic skills training gets generic results. Training anchored to a real problem — something your team is actually struggling with right now — drives engagement and retention.

"We need our staff to be more comfortable with technology" is not a problem. "Our case managers are spending 4 hours a week on documentation that AI tools could cut in half" is a problem.

**Ready:** You can name the specific task, workflow, or gap this training will address.
**Not ready yet:** The goal is vague. (Spend 30 minutes with 3 team members asking: "What takes the most time that feels like it shouldn't?" That's your problem.)

---

## Question 5: Is there psychological safety to ask basic questions?

This is the one organizations underestimate most.

Your experienced workers — especially those 50 and older — have spent decades being the expert in the room. Asking a basic technology question in front of colleagues feels like admitting incompetence. Many would rather disengage than be seen struggling.

If your team culture punishes not-knowing, AI training will produce surface-level compliance, not real skill development.

**Ready:** Your team has a culture where questions are normal and not-knowing is openly discussed.
**Not ready yet:** There's unspoken pressure to appear competent at all times. (Peer instructors and self-paced formats solve this — people will ask a peer or an AI what they won't ask a trainer.)

---

## Question 6: Do you have a way to measure results?

If you can't measure it, you can't improve it — and you can't justify continued investment.

Measurement doesn't have to be complex. Pre/post confidence surveys. Completion tracking. One qualitative conversation with 5 participants at the end. Something that tells you whether the training worked and what to do differently next time.

**Ready:** You have a simple plan for tracking completion and gathering participant feedback.
**Not ready yet:** There's no measurement plan. (Build one before you launch — it takes 30 minutes and saves you from flying blind.)

---

## Question 7: Is leadership willing to visibly participate?

This is optional — but it's a multiplier.

When a director, manager, or program lead takes the training alongside participants — even one module — it sends a signal: this matters, and nobody is above learning. That signal changes the room.

**Ready:** At least one leader is willing to go through part of the training.
**Not ready yet:** Leadership sees this as something for "the team," not for them. (Even one module attended visibly makes a difference.)

## Your Score

**5-7 YES answers:** You're ready. The conditions for a successful pilot exist. Book the consult.

**3-4 YES answers:** You're close. Identify which gaps are logistical (solvable in days) vs. cultural (solvable in weeks). Most organizations in this range can run a pilot within 30-60 days.
