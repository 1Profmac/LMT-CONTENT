Now, Lesson 2. Talk to AI.

I know this is the one people are nervous about. Here is the secret, so your shoulders can drop.

You will pick one tool. I recommend the free version of ChatGPT because it works on any device and does not require you to buy anything.

You will ask one question from your real life. You will leave with one useful answer. That is it.

Before anyone opens the tool, I need thirty seconds.

There are four things you never put into any AI tool. Not ChatGPT. Not any of them.

Your Social Security number. Never.

Your bank number, card number, or Medicare number. Never.

Your passwords or a code that arrived on your phone. Never.

Someone else's private information without their permission. Never.

Here is the only pattern you need. Three words. Situation. Ask. Format.

Situation means tell the tool who you are and what you have.

Ask means tell it exactly what you need.

Format means tell it how you want the answer to look.

Here is what that sounds like in real life.

I am 68. I cook for two. I have leftover roast chicken, rice, and spinach. Give me three easy dinners. No spicy food. For each dinner, list extra ingredients and the steps in short sentences.

That is a strong question. Not because it is long, because it is specific.

Weak is just the word dinners. Strong is who you are, what you have, what you want back.

How do you know if the answer is useful? Three tests.

I understand it. I could do the next step. Nothing private was required.

If the answer is too long, too fancy, or sounds too sure of itself, you steer it. You are not being rude. You are the one in charge here.

Four follow-ups that always work.

Make that simpler.

Give me three options.

I have 15 minutes. Cut this down.

Explain the second step like I have never done it.

And if the answer names a phone number, a dose, or uses the word always, check that part. Or throw it out. The AI can be confidently wrong. You decide if the answer is useful.

Now, Lesson 3. Don't get scammed.

I will not scare you for sport in this lesson. I will give you three skills the Federal Trade Commission and the FBI keep repeating because they work.

Then I will tell you exactly where to report a scam if one has already happened. Shame is optional. Reporting is not a confession. It is how they catch the next one.

The numbers I am about to share come from two different places. The FTC and the FBI count separately. Do not add them together.

The FTC tracks adults 60 and older. In 2024 they reported 2.4 billion dollars in losses. That is about four times the 2020 figure.

Here is the line most headlines skip. About three in four adults who reported a scam lost no money. They spotted it. That is what this lesson is for.

Skill 1. Spot a fake. Four questions. Any message. Any call.

Who started this? If you did not start the conversation, slow down. A real bank does not call you out of nowhere to confirm your account.

Does the address look odd? Look at the actual email address. Not the logo, not the name in the from field. The real address.

Are they sending you somewhere? A link you did not ask for. A QR code. A phone number they gave you. Slow down.

Would they already know your name? Dear Customer from your bank is a tell. Your bank knows your name.

One more thing. No typos is no longer a test. Scammers now use AI to write perfect English. Judge the behavior, not the spelling.

Skill 2. Name the pressure. Scammers use five levers. Once you can name them, they lose their power.

Hurry. Twenty minutes or you are arrested. Your account closes tonight. Act now.

Fear. There is a warrant. There is a virus. Your grandchild is in jail.

Secrecy. Do not tell your spouse. Do not tell the teller. This is confidential.

Authority. Badge number. Case number. Caller ID that says IRS or Social Security. A uniform is a costume.

Protect it. We need to move your money to a safe account to protect it.

Never move money to protect it. That is always the scam.

Stop if they want gift cards, a wire transfer, a crypto ATM, a courier to your door, remote control of your computer, or the code that just arrived on your phone.

Three steps. Say them with me.

Hang up.

Look up.

Call back.

Use the number on the back of your card, on your statement, or on the organization's real website. Not the number they gave you in the call or the text.

Practice saying this out loud. I am going to hang up. I will call the number on the back of my card. That sentence has ended thousands of scams.

This next one is new, and families need to hear it together.

A voice that sounds exactly like your grandchild, your son, your daughter, can now be generated from a short audio clip. The defense is simple.

Pick a family code word this week. Something you would never post online. Not a pet name. Not a birthday. Something random.

If someone calls with a voice you recognize, in a panic, asking for money, ask for the code word. If they don't know it, hang up. Then dial the number already in your phone.

Tell two people this week. Write the word on paper. Do not post it online.

Skill 3. Passwords and the second lock.

The people who set the national password rules now say longer is better. Aim for fifteen characters or more. A phrase is stronger than a symbol salad. Your email password and your bank password should never be the same word. And a notebook in a locked drawer is a completely acceptable password manager. Write them down. Lock the drawer.

Now the second lock. 2FA stands for two-factor authentication. It means password plus something you have, usually a code that arrives on your phone.

Turn it on for email first. Then your bank. Go to Settings, then Security, then look for 2-step, multifactor, or two-factor.

Never read that code to anyone. Anyone who asks for it is a scammer.

If something has already happened, here is the exact order.

Stop sending. Do not send more to prove you are cooperating.

Call your bank. Use the number on the back of your card.

Tell them exactly what you used. Wire transfer. Gift cards. Crypto ATM. Those words matter to them.

Change your email password right now. Turn on 2FA.

Report it. reportfraud.ftc.gov for the FTC. ic3.gov for the FBI. Both if you can.

Tell one trusted person. Not to be embarrassed. Because shame protects the scammer. Reporting protects the next person.

Here is the one line I want you to remember.

Unexpected, plus hurry, plus money or a code, equals hang up and verify. Every time. No exceptions.
