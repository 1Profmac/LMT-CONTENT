## SLIDE 14 — The Only Pattern You Need

Here is the only pattern you need to know. Three words.

**Situation. Ask. Format.**

Situation — tell the tool who you are and what you have.
Ask — tell it exactly what you need.
Format — tell it how you want the answer to look.

Here is what that sounds like in real life.

[Read slowly]

"I am 68. I cook for two. I have leftover roast chicken, rice, and spinach. Give me three easy dinners. No spicy food. For each dinner, list extra ingredients and the steps in short sentences."

[PAUSE]

That is a strong question. Not because it is long — because it is specific.

Weak: "dinners."
Strong: who you are, what you have, what you want back.

## SLIDE 15 — A Useful Answer and How to Steer

How do you know if the answer is useful?

Three tests. Say them with me.

I understand it.
I could do the next step.
Nothing private was required.

If the answer is too long, too fancy, or sounds too sure of itself — you steer it.

You are not being rude. You are the one in charge here.

Four follow-ups that always work:

Make that simpler.
Give me three options.
I have 15 minutes. Cut this down.
Explain the second step like I have never done it.

[PAUSE]

And if the answer names a phone number, a dose, or uses the word "always" — check that part. Or throw it out. The AI can be confidently wrong. You decide if the answer is useful.

## SLIDE 17 — The Headlines

The numbers on this screen are real — and they come from two different places. The FTC and the FBI count separately. Do not add them together.

The FTC tracks adults 60 and older. In 2024, they reported $2.4 billion in losses. That is about four times the 2020 figure.

Here is the line most headlines skip: **about three in four adults who reported a scam lost no money. They spotted it.**

That is what this lesson is for.

The FBI's number is larger — nearly five billion — because it counts more types of fraud. Investment schemes took the most. Fake tech support alone took almost a billion dollars.

The skill that stops most of this is not complicated. It is knowing what to look for.

## SLIDE 18 — Skill 1: Spot a Fake

Four questions. Any message. Any call.

**Who started this?**
If you did not start the conversation, slow down. A real bank does not call you out of nowhere to confirm your account.

**Does the address look odd?**
Look at the actual email address — not the logo, not the name in the from field. The real address.

**Are they sending you somewhere?**
A link you did not ask for. A QR code. A phone number they gave you. Slow down.

**Would they already know your name?**
"Dear Customer" from your bank is a tell. Your bank knows your name.

[PAUSE]

One more thing. No typos is no longer a test. Scammers now use AI to write perfect English. Judge the behavior, not the spelling.

---

## SLIDE 19 — Skill 2: Name the Pressure

Scammers use five levers. Once you can name them, they lose their power.

**Hurry.** Twenty minutes or you are arrested. Your account closes tonight. Act now.

**Fear.** There is a warrant. There is a virus. Your grandchild is in jail.

**Secrecy.** Do not tell your spouse. Do not tell the teller. This is confidential.

**Authority.** Badge number. Case number. Caller ID that says IRS or Social Security. A uniform is a costume.

**Protect it.** We need to move your money to a safe account to protect it.

[PAUSE]

**Never move money to protect it. That is always the scam.**

Stop if they want: gift cards, a wire transfer, a crypto ATM, a courier to your door, remote control of your computer, or the code that just arrived on your phone.

## SLIDE 20 — Hang Up. Look Up. Call Back.

Three steps. Say them out loud with me.

**Hang up.**

**Look up.**

**Call back.**

Use the number on the back of your card, on your statement, or on the organization's real website — not the number they gave you in the call or the text.

[PAUSE]

Practice saying this: "I am going to hang up. I will call the number on the back of my card."

That sentence has ended thousands of scams.

---

## SLIDE 21 — A Familiar Voice Is No Longer Proof

This one is new, and families need to hear it together.

A voice that sounds exactly like your grandchild, your son, your daughter — can now be generated with a short audio clip. AARP found that most adults our age already worry about this. The worry is correct.

The defense is simple.

Pick a family code word this week. Something you would never post on Facebook. Not a pet name. Not a birthday. Something random.

If someone calls with a voice you recognize, in a panic, asking for money — ask for the code word.

**If they don't know it — hang up. Then dial the number already in your phone.**

Tell two people this week. Write the word on paper. Do not post it online.

---

## SLIDE 22 — Skill 3: Passwords and the Second Lock

Two things. Passwords and the second lock.

**Passwords first.**

The National Institute of Standards and Technology — the people who set the rules — now say: longer is better. Aim for fifteen characters or more.

A phrase is stronger than a symbol salad. "maple-porch-radio-Thursday" is better than "P@ssw0rd1."

Your email password and your bank password should never be the same word.

And a notebook in a locked drawer is a completely acceptable password manager. Write them down. Lock the drawer.

**Now the second lock.**

2FA stands for two-factor authentication. It means: password plus something you have — usually a code that arrives on your phone.

Turn it on for email first. Then your bank. Go to Settings, then Security, then look for 2-step, multifactor, or two-factor.

**Never read that code to anyone. Anyone who asks for it is a scammer.**

## SLIDE 23 — If Money Already Moved

If something has already happened, here is the exact order.

One — stop sending. Do not send more to prove you are cooperating. Stop.

Two — call your bank. Use the number on the back of your card.

Three — tell them exactly what you used. Wire transfer. Gift cards. Crypto ATM. Those words matter to them.

Four — change your email password right now. Turn on 2FA.

Five — report it. reportfraud.ftc.gov for the FTC. ic3.gov for the FBI. Both if you can.

Six — tell one trusted person. Not to be embarrassed. Because shame protects the scammer. Reporting protects the next person.

[PAUSE]

You are not the first. You will not be the last. What you do next matters.
