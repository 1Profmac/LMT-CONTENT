Don't Get Scammed

You already did the brave part. You asked a machine a real question and stayed in charge of the answer.

This lesson is how you stay in charge when a stranger wants your fear.

I am not going to spend fifteen minutes trying to frighten you. Frightened people send money. Clear people hang up.

Three skills. That is the whole class.

1. Spot a fake
2. Name the pressure

If you walk out with those, you are already ahead of most of the country.

Those numbers are real. They are ugly. They are not a verdict on your intelligence.

Here is the line almost nobody reads out loud:

About 3 in 4 older adults who reported a scam to the FTC lost no money. They spotted it. That can be you.

The dollars exploded because a smaller number of scams now take life-changing amounts — often a hundred thousand dollars or more — through fake investments, fake banks, fake government, fake "tech support."

Phone calls still take the most money per person. The FTC's median loss on a phone-started fraud for older adults was about $2,200. If you are 80 and the phone rings, that call is still the dangerous one.

So we will not treat you like a child. We will treat you like someone who already has judgment — and who is being hunted by professionals.

STEP 1 OF 3
Spot a Fake

A fake wants you to believe the costume.

Example fake email:
From: amazon-security@email-secure-alert.com
Subject: Your account will close in 20 minutes

Look at the costume, not the logo.

The sender. Would Amazon really write from email-secure-alert.com? Hover or tap and look. Do not click the link to "check."

The greeting. "Dear Customer" from a bank that knows your name is a tell. Not always. Often.

The ask. A link to "verify." A phone number in the message. A QR code you did not ask for. A download. A request for a code that just arrived on your phone.

The surprise. You did not start this conversation. That matters more than the grammar. Plenty of scams are now well written. AI made the spelling better. Do not use "no typos" as your safety test. That test is dead.

Spot a fake — ask these four questions:
  • Who started this conversation?
  • Does the email address look odd?
  • Are they sending me to a link or number they gave me?
  • Would my bank already know my name?
The FTC will NEVER:
  • Threaten you
  • Tell you to move money to "protect it"
  • Demand gift cards or crypto

If the costume is a bank, Medicare, Amazon, Microsoft, Apple, a sheriff, a grandchild, or "tech support" that called you — treat it as a costume until you started the call on a number you already had.

How to check
Hang up. Look up. Call back.

Hang up. Look up the number on a statement, the back of the card, or the official website you type yourself. Call that number. Do not call the number in the text. That number is the costume's sleeve.

This is the Federal Trade Commission's advice, not mine. I am just unwilling to dress it up.

STEP 2 OF 3
Name the Pressure

Fakes work because of feelings, not because you cannot read. Learn the four levers.

The four pressure tactics:

Hurry. "Twenty minutes." "A warrant." "Your grandson is at the jail." Hurry is the product. If you are not allowed to think, you are not in a real official process.

Fear. Arrest. Deportation. A virus on the computer. Fear makes smart people obedient.

Secrecy. "Don't tell your wife." "Don't tell the bank teller." Isolation is the scam. A real emergency can stand a second adult.

Authority. A badge number. A case number. Caller ID that says "Austin Police" or "IRS." Caller ID is a costume. They can fake it.
The fifth lever — the one that empties retirement accounts:

"Move your money to protect it."

Someone says your account is compromised. To keep the money safe, you must wire it, buy gold, feed a crypto ATM, or hand cash to a courier. That money is not going into a vault with your name on it. It is going to them.

Combined $100,000-plus impersonation losses rose eightfold from 2020 to 2024. That is the FTC's own spotlight.
Stop if they ask for any of these:
  • Gift cards
  • Wire transfer
  • Crypto or a Bitcoin ATM
  • A courier at the door
  • Remote access to your computer

No legitimate tax office, bank, or grandchild-in-trouble story needs a Walmart gift card.

Tech support deserves its own breath. A pop-up says your computer is infected. A polite person offers to "take over the screen." Older adults are five times more likely than younger adults to report losing money on that scam, says the FTC.

If you did not call them, they do not get to drive.

The New Costume — A Voice You Love

This is the part families asked us to add. You were right to ask.

AARP found roughly eight or nine in ten adults 50+ are worried about cloned voices and deepfake video. You are not imagining the pit in your stomach.

Microsoft, AARP, and the Better Business Bureau found that scams flagged as AI-enabled — realistic voices or videos — jumped about twenty times from 2023 to 2025. Someone can call and sound like a grandchild. A few seconds of old video or voicemail can be enough.

A familiar voice is no longer proof.
Use a family code word.

Pick a word or short phrase you would never put on Facebook — "Blue canoe," the name of a dog that died years ago. Share it this week with the people you would actually send money to.

If a panicked voice asks for bail, tickets, or a gift card and cannot give the word — you hang up. Then you call your person on the number in your phone.

You are not cruel for checking. You are literate.

AARP still sees more impostors posing as companies and agencies than as family. Guard the "Amazon + bank + sheriff" pile-up too. They will transfer you from one costume to the next in a single call.

STEP 3 OF 3
Lock the Door — Passwords & 2FA

A password is a key. Most people have one key and twenty doors.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology — NIST, the people who write the grown-up rules — changed the advice a lot of us grew up with.

NIST, in plain English:

✓  Longer is better — aim for 15+ characters
✓  A phrase beats P@ssw0rd!
✓  You do not have to change it every 90 days
✓  Turn on a second lock (2FA)

A passphrase like maple-porch-radio-Thursday is stronger and kinder to your memory than Mg#9!x.

Unique matters. The password on email cannot be the password on the bank. Email is the skeleton key. If they get email, they can reset everything else.

Can't remember twenty phrases? Two honest options:

1. A password manager — a vault app — if you have someone who can sit with you once to set it up.

2. A small notebook in a drawer or a lockbox at home. Not a sticky note on the monitor. This is not shameful. A hidden notebook beats one reused password on the internet.

The Second Lock — 2FA

After your password, the site sends a code to your phone, or asks an app, or uses your face on a device you already unlocked. A thief with only the password should bounce.

CISA — the U.S. cyber defense agency — says any second factor is better than none. Still: turn it on for email and banking this week. Perfect is the enemy of a locked door.

Turn it on this week:
Email first → then the bank or credit union.

Where to find it: Settings → Security → look for "Two-factor," "2-step verification," or "Multifactor." The words change. The idea does not.
Never read a verification code to anyone.
The FTC is blunt: anyone who calls and asks you to read the code that just arrived is a scammer — even if they already know your name.

If It Already Happened

Some of you are not here for prevention. You are here because something already left the account. Listen to me.

This is not a moral failure. Professionals ran a play on a human nervous system. You are allowed to still be a competent person.

If money already moved — do these six things:

1.  Stop sending
2.  Call the bank — number on the card
3.  Tell them: wire / gift card / crypto ATM — whatever you used
4.  Change your email password. Turn on 2FA
5.  Report: ReportFraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov
6.  Tell one trusted person

Speed matters. The FTC works with the FBI's recovery team. Recovery is not a promise. Silence is a guarantee of no chance.

Identity theft steps live at identitytheft.gov.

Your Homework — Twenty Minutes

  1. Hang-up rule on the fridge. Unexpected + hurry + money or a code = hang up and verify.
  2. Family code word sent to the people who matter. Not posted online.
  3. Email 2FA turned on. Then the bank.
  4. One unique long phrase for email if it is still the same as three other sites.
  5. If a scam already happened — file the two reports today.

If you do nothing else, do the fridge line and the code word. Those two beat a weekend of worry.
