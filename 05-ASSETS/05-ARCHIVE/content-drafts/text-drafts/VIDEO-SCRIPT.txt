[TITLE CARD: The $850 Billion Cost of Ignoring Experienced Workers]
[Lower third: Brian McKinney | 50+TechBridge | learnmoretechnologies.com]

[SEGMENT 1 — THE NUMBER | ~60 seconds]
[GRAPHIC: "$850,000,000,000 / Year"]

"AARP's research puts the annual economic cost of age discrimination
in the U.S. workforce at $850 billion.

That's larger than the GDP of most countries.
It exceeds the entire U.S. Department of Defense budget.

And yet most organizations treat age discrimination as a legal compliance issue —
'don't say anything in an interview that could get us sued' —
rather than an economic one.

I spent years inside one of the largest advocacy organizations
for older Americans. I watched the policy conversations
and the real-world reality play out at the same time.

I left that world to do something about it directly.
And what I've learned since then has confirmed everything the data says."

---

[SEGMENT 2 — THE MYTH | ~90 seconds]
[GRAPHIC: "The Most Expensive Lie in Workforce Development"]

"The single most expensive lie in workforce development is this:

Older workers can't keep up with technology.

I hear it constantly. Sometimes said directly.
More often it's an unspoken assumption —
shaping hiring decisions, training budgets, promotion conversations.

The 58-year-old résumé gets passed over before the first interview.
The training budget goes to onboarding new hires —
not upskilling the 20-year veterans.

This assumption is not just wrong.
It is provably, demonstrably, empirically wrong.

I have trained Adults 50+ across 13 locations in Central Texas in digital and AI skills.

My completion rate is THREE TIMES the industry average.

In a field where online course completion rates hover around 10 to 15 percent —
50+TechBridge Pioneers complete at rates that outperform
programs designed for digital natives.

Participants report feeling significantly more confident with technology.
13 locations have invited us in.

These are not exceptional people.
They are nurses, construction supervisors, retired teachers, former caregivers.
People the workforce decided to ignore.

They are not the problem. The assumption is the problem."

---

[SEGMENT 3 — WHAT YOU'RE LOSING | ~90 seconds]
[GRAPHIC: "3 Things You're Actually Losing"]

"When an organization fails to invest in its experienced workforce,
the losses compound across three categories
that rarely appear on any budget line.

Number one: Institutional knowledge.

The 55-year-old operations manager who's leaving in 18 months
is not just taking her salary with her.
She's taking 25 years of relationship intelligence,
institutional memory, and hard-won process knowledge
that cannot be captured in an offboarding checklist.

Number two: Mentorship infrastructure.

The most effective training in any organization
is the informal mentorship that develops
when experienced workers are valued and given tools to grow.
When those workers are pushed out — that infrastructure collapses.

Number three: Consumer alignment.

Adults 50 and older control more than 70 percent
of U.S. disposable income.

If you don't employ people who understand this population from the inside —
you're building products for a market you don't understand."

---

[SEGMENT 4 — THE INVESTMENT GAP | ~60 seconds]
[GRAPHIC: "ROI Calculated Wrong"]

"Here's why the gap exists.

Training investment in America is skewed toward younger workers.

The assumption: younger workers deliver a longer return on investment.

By that logic, a 29-year-old with 3 years of experience
is a better investment than a 54-year-old with 25 years of expertise.

This is the math that produces billion-dollar workforce failures.

The research consistently shows older workers have:
— Higher job retention rates
— Stronger performance consistency
— Lower absenteeism
— Greater institutional loyalty

They are not a depreciating asset.
They are an underfunded one."

---

[SEGMENT 5 — WHAT SMART ORGS DO | ~60 seconds]
[GRAPHIC: "What's Actually Working"]

"The organizations winning the talent competition
are doing something simple:

They treat experienced workers as an asset class
that deserves the same development investment as every other talent segment.

That looks like:

Digital upskilling pathways designed for adults
who didn't grow up with smartphones —
not watered-down versions of programs built for 25-year-olds.

Bidirectional mentorship —
experienced workers teach institutional knowledge,
newer colleagues teach platform fluency.
Both directions produce measurable value.

And they're rethinking ROI timelines.

A 56-year-old who completes a 6-month digital upskilling program
has nine years until the average retirement age.
That's nine years of return on your investment."

---

[SEGMENT 6 — THE URGENCY | ~45 seconds]
[GRAPHIC: "2030: 1 in 5 Americans Will Be Over 65"]

"By 2030, one in five Americans will be over 65.

The organizations that wait for the urgency to become a crisis
before they invest — will face that crisis at scale,
without the infrastructure to respond.

The organizations that act now
will enter the next decade with a workforce advantage
their competitors will spend years trying to replicate.

This is not a DEI initiative.
This is not a feel-good program.

This is a strategic talent decision
with a quantifiable return
and a cost of inaction measured in the hundreds of billions."

---

[SEGMENT 7 — WHAT I'M SEEING | ~45 seconds]
[Return to Brian on camera]

"In libraries and senior centers and faith communities across Austin,
I watch adults 50 and older learn to use AI tools,
navigate digital platforms, and build the confidence
to apply those skills in their professional lives.

I watch people who were written off by the workforce
become competitive again.

Not because we did anything miraculous —
because we invested in them.

Twenty-three organizations have seen what happens
when someone actually commits to this population.

The results are the targeted 3X completion rate.
The increased confidence.
The Adults 50+ across 13 locations who proved the myth wrong."
